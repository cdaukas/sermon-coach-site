Where It's Strong

You refuse to make Jesus greater by leaving Moses in the dirt.

bashing someone's cherished hero is rarely the way to make them change their mind... This is not bad vs good, this is good vs better.

This is the move the whole sermon rests on, and you preached it with a rare kind of honesty. A lesser sermon would have made Jesus shine by cataloging where Moses failed, and the room would have felt the trick even if they could not name it. You did the harder and better thing. You named the shortcut out loud, you declined it, and you let the author's own strategy carry the weight: glory against greater glory, servant against son, good against better.

By guarding Moses before you elevated Christ, you did two things at once. You kept faith with the text, which never tears Moses down, and you disarmed the very people most likely to bristle, the ones for whom Moses is a cherished hero. When you say this is not bad versus good but good versus better, the listener who came in defensive can lower his guard, because you have shown him you are not out to win by diminishing what he loves. That is exegetically clean and pastorally generous in the same breath, and it set up your whole loyalty theme with integrity instead of a rhetorical shove.

Keep naming the shortcut you are declining out loud. Showing the room the move you refused is itself a lesson in how to honor what came before while still telling the truth about who is greater.

You let a non-Christian in the room follow the gospel from start to finish.

Christ has swapped places with you, so much so that when God picks up his gavel... his VERDICT IS: NOT GUILTY.

You did not leave the gospel implied or tucked between clauses where only the already-convinced would find it. You built it a scene. The courtroom sequence gives imputation a body: the gavel, the swapped places, the verdict spoken aloud. A visitor who has never heard the word imputation still walks out knowing exactly what happened, that Christ took his place and God declared him not guilty.

Then you named the mechanism plainly at the close, in Christ alone through faith alone by grace alone, so the outsider is not just moved by an image but told how the door actually opens. That pairing is what makes it land: the concrete picture makes the truth felt, and the plain statement keeps it from dissolving into mere feeling.

Keep doing exactly this. When you tie the declarative gospel to a picture the whole room can see, you make imputation something people watch happen rather than a doctrine they are asked to accept.

You grow the redemptive arc out of the text instead of bolting it on.

Moses pointed forward by giving us a law that we couldn't keep, Jesus is the Son who keeps the law in our place and we are the house that he has inherited by his blood

Some sermons reach for Christ at the end with a verse from somewhere else, as if the passage in front of them could not get there on its own. You let this text preach Christ from its own logic. Moses gives the law we cannot keep, Jesus keeps it in our place, and we are the house he inherited by his blood. That is a clean promise-and-fulfillment move, and it means your gospel is not an add-on but the destination the passage was already traveling toward.

Just as important, you kept landing the trouble, our wavering loyalty when following costs us, on Christ's finished faithfulness rather than on our own resolve. That is the difference between a sermon that leaves people trying harder and one that leaves them resting in what has already been done.

Keep letting the house metaphor carry the gospel weight the way you did here. When the text is already preaching Christ, you do not need a second illustration to help it along.

How To Grow

Give the present-day pull the same concrete naming you gave the original audience.

You drew the Hebrews' temptation with real precision, the pressure to slide back to the synagogue, to the easier and more respectable road. But when you turned to your own people, the pull softened into some other way is just easier, and the call became keep considering Jesus, don't ever stop. That is true, but it floats above the room. You have already proved you can name a specific loyalty. Your Bears opening did it, and your distraction list did it. The closing deserves the same eye.

This week
Write down three weekday moments where someone in your congregation actually chooses the easier road: the promotion that quietly moves faith to the periphery, the weekend at the lake instead of the gathering, the relationship where the biblical view costs a friendship. Then preach keep considering Jesus into one of those named scenes instead of into the general air. And do not forget the person who is not drifting but doubting, the one wondering whether Christ is worth the cost at all. Name his situation too, and the call stops being a slogan and becomes a hand on a specific shoulder.

What It Looks Like

Before

Keep considering Jesus. Don't ever stop.

After

So keep considering Jesus, and don't ever stop. But let me put that where you actually live. Some of you got the promotion this year, and somewhere in the busy of it your faith slid to the edge of the desk. Some of you have a friendship that would cost you something the moment you said what Scripture actually says. That is the easier road these believers were tempted toward, dressed in your clothes. Consider Jesus right there, in the promotion, in that conversation. He is the Son who kept faith all the way to the blood, and he did not consider you from a safe distance.

The call now lands on a named weekday scene instead of the general air, and it still ends on what Christ has done rather than on the listener's resolve.
