What's solid2 of 6

The gospel turn is doing real work. "He didn't just bring the necessary sacrifices for us, HE HIMSELF BECAME THE NECESSARY SACRIFICE FOR US" is the moment the sermon stops describing Jesus and starts giving him away. You ground it in the text, verse 2, and then you press it into the ache: "most everyone around you lets you down... but Jesus never let God the Father down." That is the sound of advice becoming good news. Protect it.

The big idea and the ending are the same sentence, on purpose. "Keep your confidence squarely placed on Jesus" opens the sermon and closes it, and the closing does not drift off to a new subject. The last ninety seconds land exactly where the big idea pointed. That is harder to pull off than it looks, and you did it.

The close widens its address without losing its aim. You speak to the drifting believer, "are you near to Jesus?", and then you open the door to the one who isn't in the house yet, and both invitations run on the same gospel: in Christ alone, through faith alone. That is not two sermons. That is one sermon reaching two rows of the same room. Keep it.

The one thing to findBefore Sunday

The hub is the change you're preaching toward. Look at what you wrote in THE ONE THING and then look at where the last ninety seconds actually land, because they are aiming at different things.

The Monday change you named is active: "make the hard decisions that loyalty require." That is a move. A person could walk out and do something. But then the sermon closes on a different verb. Your final application reads: "The more you honestly consider Jesus, the more superior you will see him to be. Keep considering Jesus." That is not a decision. That is a posture, keep looking, keep pondering, keep considering. The ache you opened with is someone questioning whether loyalty is worth it, and the close answers "consider harder and you'll be convinced." But you promised, in your own words, that they'd walk out and make a hard decision. The sermon builds toward a choice and then lands on a contemplation.

So the change disagrees with the ending, and it disagrees with the ache too. The ache is about the cost of loyalty, worth it or not. "Keep considering Jesus" doesn't cost anything by Monday. It's true, and it belongs in the sermon, but it is the fuel, not the decision. If a man is wondering whether following Jesus is worth what it's taking from him, "consider Jesus more" tells him where confidence comes from but never names the thing he has to actually do about it.

Here's what it costs you if you leave it. The person who is genuinely on the fence, drifting, choosing the easier good things, will hear a moving call to look at Jesus, feel stirred, and walk out with nothing to do on Monday except feel stirred again. You already know the harder decision is in there, because you named it. The sermon just doesn't ask for it out loud at the end.

The question to settle

What is the one hard decision of loyalty you want someone to actually make this week, and does your ending ask for it, or does it stop at "keep considering"?

Two smaller things

The escape room may cost you your last thirty seconds. It's a warm, real image, but it arrives in the highest-stakes moment of the sermon and it's playful right where you want weight. Watch that it doesn't discharge the tension you built with "a boast that can't be stolen from you, not even in death." That line is stronger than the escape room. Make sure the room serves it and doesn't upstage it.

Point one is thin on the page. "Our confidence is bolstered by our identity" is a real move from Hebrews 3:1, "holy brothers, you who share in a heavenly calling", but you gave it almost nothing here while point two carries the whole gospel turn. As you write toward the manuscript, watch that identity gets enough weight to earn being point one, or it becomes a heading you pass through on the way to Jesus.

This is a pre-read before you write, while the fixes are still cheap to change. When it's written, run the finished manuscript through the full evaluation.
